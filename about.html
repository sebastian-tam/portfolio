My Story
The Ultra-Marathoner

For over 20 years, I have participated in more than 50 ultra-marathons and marathons across some of the world's most unforgiving landscapes — scorching deserts, high-altitude mountain trails, and technical terrain that demands every ounce of mental and physical reserve you possess.

Ultra-running is not simply sport. It is a school of thought. When you are 40 miles into a 100-mile race and your legs have stopped responding to rational commands, the only thing that keeps you moving is a deeply held conviction that the work done before the race — the unglamorous, unseen preparation — will carry you through. That conviction, earned mile by mile over two decades, has become the lens through which I approach every business challenge I take on.

From the deserts of the Middle East to the mountain ranges of Europe and Asia, these races have taught me that the difference between finishing and quitting is almost never physical. It is almost always a question of how clearly you can see your goal when everything is telling you to stop.

The Business Philosophy

Long-Term Pacing
Ultra-running teaches you to pace for the finish, not the mile marker. In startups, this translates to building sustainable revenue systems — not chasing short-term spikes that destroy long-term margins.
Grit Through the Plateau
Every growth curve has a plateau that feels like failure. Every ultra has a point at mile 60 that feels like the end. The skill is recognising the difference between a signal to adjust and a signal to keep going.
Data-Driven Execution
The best ultra-runners obsess over split times, elevation data, and nutrition metrics. I bring the same quantitative rigour to growth — measuring everything from ML fare prediction MAPE to conversion funnel drop-offs.
Series A to IPO Runway
Taking a company from early-stage to IPO is not a sprint. It requires the same sequential, disciplined commitment as training across 20 years of ultra-events — each one building the foundation for what comes next.
"The finish line is never the hardest part. The hardest part is showing up the next morning, after the race is over, and doing the work that no one sees. That's where growth — in running and in business — is actually built."
— Sebastian Tam
